B2C vs B2B BSS Architecture

Account hierarchies, comparison table, and architecture differences

B2B (Business-to-Business) BSS is architecturally distinct from B2C (Business-to-Consumer) BSS. Enterprise customers have different needs: hierarchical account structures, contract-first ordering, solution-level quotes, complex approval workflows, and multi-site service management. This section highlights the key differences.

B2C vs B2B — Key Differences

DimensionB2C (Consumer)B2B (Enterprise / Business)
Customer entityIndividual personOrganisation with hierarchy (HQ, subsidiaries, sites)
Account structureSingle accountHierarchical accounts (billing, service, sub-accounts)
Order triggerCustomer initiates orderContract signed first, then order from contract
Quote complexitySimple (single product, standard price)Complex (multi-product, multi-site, recurring + one-time, discounts, bundles)
PricingStandard, non-negotiableNegotiated, customer-specific, contract-bound
Approval workflowNone or simple credit checkMulti-level (sales manager, legal, finance, credit)
Service locationsSingle (home address)Multiple sites, each with separate services
BillingSimple (single account, one invoice)Complex (hierarchical billing, split invoices, consolidated billing)
Contract managementTerms & conditions accepted onlineNegotiated contract, SLAs, termination clauses, auto-renewal
ProvisioningSimple (activate single line)Complex (multi-site, multi-domain, coordinated activation)
Customer inventoryTrack what customer hasTrack contract entitlements, consumed services, available inventory

Account Hierarchy Model

B2B BSS must support hierarchical account structures to model enterprise customer relationships:

  • Billing Account — The entity responsible for payment. May be at HQ level.
  • Service Account — The entity that uses the service. May differ from billing account.
  • Site Account — A physical location where services are delivered. Each site may have multiple services.
  • Contact — Individuals associated with the account (technical contact, billing contact, administrator).

Contract-First Ordering Flow

Unlike B2C where the order leads to the contract, B2B follows a contract-first model:

  1. Opportunity — Sales team identifies enterprise opportunity.
  2. Solution Design — CPQ creates a solution-level quote with multiple products across multiple sites.
  3. Approval — Credit, legal, and technical feasibility approvals.
  4. Contract — Signed contract captures terms, SLA, pricing, duration, early termination clauses.
  5. Order(s) — One or more orders are created from the contract for initial provisioning and subsequent changes.
  6. Fulfilment — COM/SOM/XDO fulfil the order across network domains.
  7. Billing — Billing is set up based on contract terms (recurring + one-time, discounts, credits).

Impact on BSS Systems

  • CRM — Must support hierarchical accounts, multiple contacts, and contract history.
  • CPQ — Must support solution-level quoting across sites and products (see 12.2).
  • COM — Must support contract-based ordering and partial fulfilment (some sites active, others pending).
  • Billing — Must support hierarchical billing, invoice consolidation, and customer-specific pricing.
  • CLM — Often a separate system that manages the contract lifecycle (see 12.3).

Key Takeaway

The fundamental architectural difference between B2C and B2B BSS is the shift from order-first to contract-first. In B2B, the contract is the master record — it defines what the customer is entitled to, at what price, for how long, and with what SLAs. Orders are derivative of the contract. BSS systems that treat orders as the primary entity will struggle to support B2B requirements without significant customisation.
